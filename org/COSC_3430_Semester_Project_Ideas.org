* Overview

In a team of 2-3, you'll pick one project (build or concept) and carry
it through a semester-long scrum-style sprint structure with four
sprint reviews, on the same dates for everyone in the class:

- *Sprint 1 — Proposal:* propose your project's scope
- *Sprint 2 — Literature Review:* research the algorithm(s)/approach(es)
  you'll use
- *Sprint 3 — Results:* build and test your MVP (Minimum Viable Product)
- *Sprint 4 — Discussion/Presentation:* demo your project and reflect on it

  Below are ten ideas for *build* projects. Each ties directly to a
  specific chapter (or pair of chapters) of Géron's /Hands-On Machine
  Learning/ — you'll be able to build these using techniques covered in
  class. Scope down further if needed — a working, well-understood
  small system beats an ambitious, half-understood one.

  Instead of a build project, you could also elect to complete a
  *concept* project. This requires picking one of the big course topics,
  finding an interesting real-world application of it, or dig deep
  into one or more existing Python packages, and explaining it to the
  class, ideally with an interactive demonstration.

  These are just ideas. You can also pick a coding problem or an
  application yourself — just clear it with me first (via the project
  proposal).

  *A note on sources:* every idea below is grounded specifically in
  Géron's /Hands-On Machine Learning with Scikit-Learn and PyTorch/
  (2025). I have not drawn on any other textbook for this
  document. When you research or build, always name your source — if
  you bring in an idea, technique, or example from somewhere else (a
  blog post, a paper, another textbook, Stack Overflow), say so
  explicitly. Don't blend an unnamed outside source into something
  that looks like it came from Géron.

  *A note on grading philosophy:* because this is a lecture-only course
  and I haven't built or solved these specific projects myself, I've
  picked ideas that are either self-checking (there's a clear
  correct/incorrect outcome — an accuracy number, a
  working/non-working system) or that naturally support a "naive
  version vs. improved version" comparison (e.g., a single decision
  tree vs. a random forest). Favor a project you can evaluate
  quantitatively over one that depends on subjective judgment of "how
  good" your solution is.

* Project Ideas (Build/Coding)

** 1. Regression Project: Predict a Real-World Number, End to End

Pick a small tabular regression dataset from a domain that is *not*
housing prices — e.g., bike-share ride counts, restaurant tips,
diamond prices, or a small Kaggle dataset of your choice — and walk
it through the machine learning project workflow: frame the
problem, explore the data, prepare it, train a baseline model, then
improve it with regularization.

- *MVP:* one dataset, train/test split, a plain linear regression
  baseline, then a regularized model (ridge or lasso). Report and compare
  RMSE on the test set. No need for the full "launch and monitor" stage
  of the workflow — stop once you can defend your final RMSE.

- *Naive vs. improved:* plain linear regression vs. regularized
  regression — a lower test RMSE is your evidence the improvement
  worked.

- *Source:* Géron Ch. 1 (framing the problem), Ch. 2 (the end-to-end
  project workflow), Ch. 4 (training models).

** 2. Classification on an Imbalanced or Small Tabular Dataset

Pick a binary classification dataset that is *not* MNIST — e.g., a small
medical diagnosis dataset (like the Wisconsin breast cancer dataset),
mushroom edibility, or a churn/loan-default dataset — and build a
classifier, evaluated the way Géron insists you must: not just
accuracy.

- *MVP:* train/test split, one classifier (logistic regression or SGD),
  confusion matrix, precision, recall, and an ROC curve. Compare your
  model's accuracy against a "majority class" baseline (always predict
  the most common label).

- *Self-checking:* your model should visibly beat the majority-class
  baseline, and you should be able to say *why* precision or recall
  matters more for your chosen dataset (e.g., missing a cancer case is
  worse than a false alarm).

- *Source:* Géron Ch. 3 (classification, confusion matrices,
  precision/recall, ROC).

** 3. Image Classifier for a Non-MNIST Image Dataset

Build a multiclass image classifier for a small, non-MNIST,
non-Fashion- MNIST image dataset — e.g., Kuzushiji-MNIST (historical
Japanese script), EMNIST letters, or a small Kaggle image set. Compare
a simple baseline classifier against a scikit-learn MLP.

- *MVP:* load a small (≤ ~20k image) dataset, train a k-nearest-neighbors
  or SGD baseline, then an ~MLPClassifier~, report test accuracy for
  both.

- *Naive vs. improved:* KNN/SGD baseline vs. MLP — a real, reportable
  accuracy gap.

- *Source:* Géron Ch. 3 (classification techniques), Ch. 9 (MLPs for
  image classification — modeled on Géron's own Fashion MNIST example,
  but you must use a *different* image dataset).

** 4. Decision Tree vs. Random Forest on a High-Stakes Tabular Dataset

Pick a tabular dataset where a wrong prediction obviously matters — e.g.,
mushroom edibility (edible/poisonous) or a loan-default dataset — and
compare a single decision tree against a random forest.

- *MVP:* train one ~DecisionTreeClassifier~ and one
  ~RandomForestClassifier~ on the same train/test split. Report accuracy
  for both, and retrain the single tree 3–5 times with different random
  seeds to show how much a single tree's structure and accuracy can
  swing — then show the forest is more stable.

- *Self-checking:* mushroom edibility especially gives you a hard,
  unambiguous correctness check.

- *Source:* Géron Ch. 5 (decision trees, variance across retrains),
  Ch. 6 (random forests).

** 5. Ensemble Showdown: Voting vs. Bagging vs. Boosting

Pick a tabular classification dataset different from the one you used in
Idea 4, and compare three ensemble strategies head to head.

- *MVP:* train 2–3 diverse base classifiers, combine them with a
  ~VotingClassifier~ (soft voting), separately train a
  ~BaggingClassifier~, and separately train an ~AdaBoostClassifier~ or
  gradient boosting model. Report all four accuracies (three
  ensembles + best individual base classifier) in one table.

- *Self-checking:* does any ensemble actually beat the best individual
  classifier? (Sometimes the honest answer is "barely" — that's a
  fine, defensible result too.)

- *Source:* Géron Ch. 6 (voting classifiers, bagging, boosting).

** 6. K-Means Color Segmentation Tool with Automatic K Selection

Build a small tool that takes any photo you supply, runs k-means color
segmentation on it, and picks a reasonable number of clusters
automatically instead of you guessing.

- *MVP:* run k-means color segmentation (Géron's approach) for k = 2
  through 12 on one image, plot the inertia (within-cluster sum of
  squares) against k, and use the "elbow" to pick a final k
  automatically. Show the segmented image at the naive default (e.g.,
  k=8, fixed) next to the image at your chosen k.

- *Naive vs. improved:* a fixed, arbitrary k vs. an elbow-selected k —
  defend your choice with the inertia plot.

- *Source:* Géron Ch. 8 (k-means for image segmentation). Use your own
  photo, not the book's ladybug image.

** 7. Dimensionality Reduction Visualizer for a Non-MNIST Dataset

Pick a dataset with many features (not MNIST/digits) — e.g., a wine
quality or gene-expression-style tabular dataset with 10+ features, or a
small non-MNIST image set — and build a small tool that visualizes it in
2D using PCA and t-SNE side by side, colored by class label.

- *MVP:* compute the number of PCA components needed to preserve 95% of
  variance, project to 2D with PCA, separately project to 2D with t-SNE,
  and plot both, colored by class.

- *Self-checking:* train a simple classifier (e.g., logistic regression)
  on the raw features vs. on just the 2 PCA components, and compare
  accuracy — this quantifies how much the 2D projection actually lost.

- *Source:* Géron Ch. 7 (PCA, choosing the number of dimensions, t-SNE).

** 8. Semi-Supervised Labeling: How Far Can Clustering Stretch a Few Labels?

Simulate a "mostly unlabeled" situation: take a labeled dataset, keep
only a small number of true labels (e.g., 50 out of thousands), and use
clustering to propagate labels to the rest.

- *MVP:* cluster the unlabeled data with k-means, propagate each
  cluster's most common known label to all its members, then train a
  classifier on the expanded label set. Compare its test accuracy
  against a classifier trained on just the original 50 labeled
  examples.

- *Self-checking:* label-propagation should measurably beat the
  tiny-labeled-set baseline — if it doesn't, that's still a valid,
  explainable result.

- *Source:* Géron Ch. 8 (clustering for semi-supervised learning). §Use
  a dataset other than the ~digits~ dataset Géron uses for this
  technique.

** 9. From Scikit-Learn MLP to PyTorch MLP

Pick a tabular dataset (not California housing, not CoverType — both are
Géron's own examples) and build the *same* MLP architecture twice: once
with scikit-learn's ~MLPRegressor~/~MLPClassifier~, once by hand in
PyTorch following Géron's Ch. 10 approach.

- *MVP:* one dataset, one fixed architecture (e.g., 2 hidden layers of a
  given size), trained both ways. Report test performance for both and
  the wall-clock training time for both.

- *Self-checking:* the two versions should reach roughly comparable
  performance — if they don't, that's a debugging exercise worth
  discussing at Sprint 4.

- *Source:* Géron Ch. 9 (MLPs with scikit-learn), Ch. 10 (building the
  same kind of network in PyTorch).

** 10. Shallow CNN vs. Deeper Regularized CNN

Pick a small image classification dataset (not MNIST, not CIFAR-10, not
ImageNet — all are Géron's or the field's standard running examples) and
compare a naive shallow CNN against a deeper one that uses the training
tricks from Ch. 11.

- *MVP:* train a shallow CNN (1–2 conv layers) as your naive baseline,
  then a deeper CNN (3–4 conv layers) that adds batch normalization
  and/or dropout. Report test accuracy for both and a training/validation
  loss curve for both.

- *Naive vs. improved:* shallow vs. deeper+regularized — and if the
  deeper model *doesn't* win, that's a legitimate and interesting
  finding to present (it happens, especially on small datasets).

- *Source:* Géron Ch. 11 (training deep networks: batch norm, dropout),
  Ch. 12 (convolutional layers, CNN architecture).

* Project Ideas (Concept/Explaining)

Pick one topic below, find a real-world application of it, or dig deep
into one or more existing Python packages, and explain it to the class
— ideally with an interactive or live demo, not just a
summary. "Explaining it to the class" means more than reciting what
the book says: show a worked example, run a live or recorded demo, and
be ready to answer questions about how it actually works under the
hood.

These are just examples - you can pick another concept from the book
that we did not already cover in depth in class (that includes almost
everything). The point is not to be original but go deep.

** The ML Landscape (Ch. 1)

- *Why your streaming app's recommendations "learn" instead of running
  on hardcoded rules* — explain the difference between instance-based
  and model-based learning using recommendation systems as the
  example, and demo a tiny content-based "if you liked X, you might
  like Y" similarity recommender built on a handful of items you
  define.

** The ML Project Workflow (Ch. 2)

- *How a real pricing or estimation tool gets built, end to end* — walk
  through Géron's eight-step ML project checklist (frame the problem,
  get the data, explore, prepare, select and train, fine-tune,
  present, launch/monitor) using a *different* domain and dataset than
  the book's housing example — e.g., a small dataset predicting flight
  delays, movie ratings, or restaurant wait times — and demo at least
  the explore-and-prepare steps live on real data.

** Classification Metrics (Ch. 3)

- *Why "99% accurate" fraud detection can still be a bad model* —
  explain precision, recall, and the accuracy trap on wildly
  imbalanced data (fraud, rare disease screening, spam), and demo a
  confusion matrix and precision-recall curve on a small imbalanced
  dataset, showing how a "predict everything is normal" model gets
  high accuracy and is useless.

** Gradient Descent (Ch. 4)

- *How your phone's GPS or a fitness tracker fits a smooth line through
  noisy sensor data* — explain gradient descent as the general
  mechanism behind fitting a model to noisy data, and demo an
  interactive visualization where the learning rate is a slider: show
  convergence at a good learning rate and divergence at too high a
  learning rate.

** Decision Trees & Ensembles (Ch. 5–6)

- *Why medical and financial risk tools use "committees" of models, not
  one* — explain why a single decision tree is unstable (high variance)
  and how averaging many trees (a random forest) fixes that, and demo
  retraining a single tree on the same data with different random
  seeds to visually show how much its structure changes, next to a
  stable random forest.

** Dimensionality Reduction (Ch. 7)

- *How a music app compresses "taste" into a handful of numbers* —
  explain PCA as compressing many correlated features (genre signals,
  tempo, energy, etc.) into a few components that capture most of the
  variance, and demo an explained-variance plot plus a 2D PCA
  projection of a small features dataset, colored by genre or mood
  label.

** Clustering (Ch. 8)

- *How photo apps group your camera roll without being told the
  categories* — explain unsupervised clustering (k-means or DBSCAN) as
  grouping-without-labels, and demo clustering a small set of images
  or feature vectors, discussing what happens when you pick k too
  small or too large.

** Artificial Neural Networks (Ch. 9)

- *What's actually happening inside a "neural network" your phone's
  keyboard uses to guess your next word or recognize your handwriting*
  — explain the MLP as a stack of weighted sums and activation
  functions, nothing more mysterious than that, and demo training a
  tiny MLP live on a simple 2D dataset, showing its decision boundary
  change as training progresses (similar in spirit to the "neural
  network playground" tool Géron points to — cite it explicitly if you
  use it, don't just reproduce the exercise as if it were new).

** Deep Computer Vision / CNNs (Ch. 11–12)

- *How a photo app finds edges, faces, and objects starting from raw
  pixels* — explain convolution as a small sliding pattern-detector
  with shared weights, and demo visualizing what the first-layer
  filters of a small CNN you train actually detect (edges, color
  blobs) by displaying their learned kernels or the feature maps they
  produce on a sample image.

** Transformers and LLMs (Ch. 15)

- *How to Fine-Tuning a Pretrained Transformer for a Text*. Pick a text
  classification or generation task on a domain-specific dataset
  (e.g., product reviews, movie plot summaries, or a small custom text
  corpus you assemble yourself) and fine-tune a small pretrained
  transformer model from Hugging Face on it, comparing the fine-tuned
  model's performance against a simple non-transformer baseline (e.g.,
  logistic regression on TF-IDF features) to see how much the
  pretrained language representation actually buys you.

* AI Use Policy
AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, GitHub Copilot, etc.) are permitted on this
project under the following rules:

*Allowed without restriction:*
- Sprint 2 (Literature Review) research
- Debugging help — understanding an error message or why your code
  doesn't behave as expected
- Explaining unfamiliar syntax or library behavior

  *Allowed with disclosure:*
  - Using AI to generate starter code or a first draft of a method — but
    you must be able to explain and reproduce the logic yourself, without
    the AI present

  *Not allowed:*
  - Submitting AI-generated code you don't understand as final work
  - Using AI to complete the Sprint 3 (Results) MVP end-to-end — this
    sprint exists to show what *you* can build

  *Disclosure requirement:*
  Submit a short AI-use log with your Sprint 3 and Sprint 4 deliverables:
  what tool you used, for what purpose, and what you changed or corrected.
  Reasonable, disclosed use is not penalized.

  *Spot-check:*
  During Sprint 4, you may be asked to explain or modify any part of your
  codebase live. Be ready to walk through your own code.
